## Section 1: Foundations of Financial Accounting & Conceptual Framework

### 1.1 Definition, Objectives, and Core Activities of Accounting
Accounting is an information system that identifies, records, and communicates the economic events of an organization to interested users. It serves as the primary language of business, transforming raw operational data into structured financial intelligence for decision-making.

The accounting process consists of three core activities:
1. **Identification**: Selecting economic events (transactions) relevant to a specific business entity. Examples include paying employee wages, purchasing inventory, rendering services to clients, or acquiring machinery.
2. **Recording**: Keeping a systematic, chronological, and quantified diary of identified economic events in monetary terms. This involves journalizing transactions, posting to ledger accounts, and maintaining internal ledgers.
3. **Communication**: Preparing structured financial reports (financial statements) and interpreting the results through ratio analysis, trend evaluation, and managerial reporting.

#### Accounting vs. Bookkeeping
Bookkeeping is strictly the mechanical, procedural recording aspect of accounting. Accounting encompasses the broader framework—identifying transactions, designing accounting systems, analyzing financial reports, auditing, tax planning, and strategic management consulting.

```
+------------------+      +------------------+      +------------------+
|  IDENTIFICATION  | ---> |    RECORDING     | ---> |  COMMUNICATION   |
| Select Economic  |      | Systematic Diary |      | Financial Statements|
|     Events       |      | in Money Terms   |      |  & Analysis      |
+------------------+      +------------------+      +------------------+
```

### 1.2 Users of Accounting Information & Disclosure Framework
Financial accounting information serves two distinct categories of decision-makers:

#### Internal Users
Managers, executives, department heads, production supervisors, and human resource directors who plan, organize, and run business operations.
* **Information Needs**: Cost per unit, cash projections, profit margins by product line, sales forecasts, and inventory management.
* **Reporting Tools**: Special-purpose internal management reports, budget performance summaries, and cost-volume-profit analyses.

#### External Users
Individuals and entities outside the organization who make financial decisions regarding the business entity.
* **Investors (Owners)**: Evaluate whether to buy, hold, or sell ownership shares based on historical profitability and return on equity.
* **Creditors (Suppliers, Banks)**: Assess creditworthiness, solvency, and debt-paying ability before extending loans or commercial credit.
* **Tax Authorities (IRS, State Revenue Agencies)**: Verify compliance with tax laws and taxable income calculations.
* **Regulatory Agencies (SEC, IASB)**: Ensure full disclosure and compliance with statutory financial reporting standards.

#### Mandatory vs. Voluntary Disclosure
* **Mandatory Disclosure**: Required by statute or accounting regulatory bodies (e.g., quarterly 10-Q reports, audited annual 10-K statements, GAAP-compliant balance sheets).
* **Voluntary Disclosure**: Optional financial and non-financial data released by management to enhance market reputation (e.g., corporate social responsibility reports, 5-year strategic outlooks).

### 1.3 Standard-Setting Bodies & The Regulatory Environment
To maintain consistency and comparability across financial reports, standardized rules govern financial accounting:

* **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**: The primary private-sector organization establishing Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) in the United States.
* **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**: An agency of the U.S. federal government that holds statutory authority to regulate financial reporting for publicly traded corporations. The SEC grants FASB the primary role in establishing accounting standards while retaining oversight authority.
* **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)**: An independent standard-setting body based in London that issues International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), used by over 100 countries worldwide.
* **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**: The collective set of standards, principles, and assumptions universally practiced in the U.S. to ensure relevance and faithful representation.

### 1.4 Qualitative Characteristics of Accounting Information
Useful accounting information must satisfy two primary qualitative characteristics:

1. **Relevance**: Information is relevant if it can make a difference in a business decision. Relevant information possesses:
   * **Predictive Value**: Helps users forecast future outcomes.
   * **Confirmatory Value**: Confirms or corrects prior expectations.
   * **Materiality**: An item is material if its omission or misstatement could influence decision-makers.
2. **Faithful Representation**: Information accurately depicts the real-world economic substance of transactions. Faithful representation requires information to be:
   * **Complete**: No essential data omitted.
   * **Neutral**: Free from bias or selective manipulation.
   * **Free from Material Error**: Mathematically and factually accurate.

### 1.5 Measurement Principles: Historical Cost vs. Fair Value
GAAP generally utilizes two measurement principles to value assets and liabilities:

#### Historical Cost Principle (Cost Principle)
Dictates that companies record assets at their initial acquisition cost. This cost remains the reported valuation throughout the asset's holding period, regardless of changes in market value.
* **Advantage**: Verifiable, objective, factual, and faithfully representative.
* **Example**: If Best Buy purchases land for \$300,000, it is recorded at \$300,000. Even if the market value of the land increases to \$400,000 the following year, it remains reported on the Balance Sheet at \$300,000 under historical cost GAAP.

#### Fair Value Principle
States that assets and liabilities should be reported at fair value—the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to settle a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants.
* **Advantage**: Highly relevant for fast-moving financial instruments.
* **Application**: Applied primarily to actively traded investment securities (such as stocks and bonds) where market price quotes are readily available and verifiable.

#### The Relevance vs. Faithful Representation Trade-Off
Most non-financial assets (land, buildings, equipment) follow the historical cost principle because fair values are subjective and volatile. Fair value is restricted to assets with active, liquid markets.

### 1.6 Core Accounting Assumptions

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    FOUNDATIONAL ACCOUNTING ASSUMPTIONS                 |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  MONETARY UNIT     |  Includes only transaction data that can be       |
|    ASSUMPTION      |  expressed in monetary terms (\$ / € / £).         |
+--------------------+--------------------------------------------------+
|  ECONOMIC ENTITY   |  Activities of the business must be kept         |
|    ASSUMPTION      |  separate from the owner's personal affairs.     |
+--------------------+--------------------------------------------------+
|    TIME PERIOD     |  The life of a business can be divided into      |
|    ASSUMPTION      |  artificial reporting periods (months/years).    |
+--------------------+--------------------------------------------------+
|   GOING CONCERN    |  Assumes the enterprise will operate for the     |
|    ASSUMPTION      |  foreseeable future without liquidation.         |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

1. **Monetary Unit Assumption**: Requires that companies include in accounting records only transaction data capable of being expressed in terms of money.
   * *Limitation*: Non-quantifiable factors such as owner health, employee morale, service quality, and brand reputation cannot be included on the Balance Sheet.
2. **Economic Entity Assumption**: Requires that activities of an economic entity be kept separate and distinct from the personal activities of its owner(s) and all other economic entities.
   * **Proprietorship**: Business owned by one individual. Small retail/service firm; legal liability is personal, but accounting records are strictly separate.
   * **Partnership**: Business owned by two or more persons. Shared profits and joint personal liability.
   * **Corporation**: Business organized as a separate legal entity under state corporation law, with ownership divided into transferable shares of stock. Limited liability for stockholders.
3. **Time Period Assumption (Periodicity)**: States that the economic life of a business can be divided into artificial time periods (monthly, quarterly, or annually). The standard financial reporting period is one fiscal year.
4. **Going Concern Assumption**: Assumes that the business entity will continue operating in the foreseeable future rather than face imminent liquidation. This justifies carrying plant assets at historical cost minus depreciation rather than liquidation value.

* **Key Stakeholders Affected**: Ken Mason (professional integrity/job security), Rick Payne (executive reputation), potential investors (financial loss risk), and Lions Company (legal liability and loss of market credibility).

## Section 2: The Accounting Equation & Transaction Analysis Framework

### 2.1 The Basic Accounting Equation
The fundamental mathematical relationship governing financial accounting is:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Equity}$$

This equation must balance after **every single economic transaction**. The left side represents total economic resources owned; the right side represents the total claims against those resources by creditors and owners.

---

### 2.2 The Expanded Accounting Equation
To capture the full dynamic impact of business operations, Owner's Equity is expanded into its four sub-components:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Capital} - \text{Owner's Drawings} + \text{Revenues} - \text{Expenses}$$

```
                                  +---------------------------------------------------+
                                  |                  OWNER'S EQUITY                   |
                                  +---------------------------------------------------+
                                  |  Owner's Capital  |  Investments (+)              |
                                  |  Owner's Drawings |  Personal Withdrawals (-)     |
                                  |  Revenues         |  Operating Income (+)         |
                                  |  Expenses         |  Operating Costs (-)          |
                                  +---------------------------------------------------+
```

---

### 2.3 Comprehensive Element Definitions

#### 1. Assets
Resources owned by a business that possess future economic value and probable future benefits.
* **Cash**: Currency, bank deposits, physical money on hand.
* **Accounts Receivable**: Claims against customers for cash due from services rendered or goods sold on open account.
* **Supplies**: Consumable office or operational materials on hand for short-term use.
* **Prepaid Insurance / Rent**: Future economic benefits paid in cash in advance before consumption (Current Asset).
* **Equipment / Machinery / Vehicles / Land / Buildings**: Long-term tangible physical assets used in business operations.

#### 2. Liabilities
Debts and economic obligations owed to creditors (external parties).
* **Accounts Payable**: Obligations to pay suppliers for goods or services purchased on open credit terms.
* **Notes Payable**: Formal written promissory note obligations payable to banks or creditors (often interest-bearing).
* **Salaries and Wages Payable**: Obligations owed to employees for services already performed but not yet paid.
* **Unearned Service Revenue**: Liability created when cash is received from customers in advance before services are performed.

#### 3. Owner's Equity
The residual ownership claim on total assets after deducting total liabilities ($\text{Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}$).
* **Owner's Capital (+)**: Contributions of cash or assets made into the business by the proprietor.
* **Owner's Drawings (-)**: Withdrawals of cash or assets by the owner for personal living use. Represents a disinvestment, **never an expense**.
* **Revenues (+)**: Gross increases in owner's equity resulting from business activities for the purpose of earning income (Service Revenue, Sales Revenue, Rent Revenue, Interest Revenue).
* **Expenses (-)**: Costs of assets consumed or services used in the process of generating revenue (Salaries Expense, Rent Expense, Utilities Expense, Advertising Expense, Supplies Expense, Maintenance Expense).

## Section 3: Financial Statements — Comprehensive Preparation & Dr. Nadia's Strict Rules

### 3.1 The Four Financial Statements & Sequence of Preparation
Companies prepare four formal financial statements from summarized accounting data in a strict sequential order:

```
+---------------------+     +---------------------+     +---------------------+     +---------------------+
|  INCOME STATEMENT   | --> |   OWNER'S EQUITY    | --> |    BALANCE SHEET    | --> | STATEMENT OF CASH   |
| Revenues - Expenses |     |      STATEMENT      |     |  Assets = Liab. +   |     |        FLOWS        |
|    = Net Income     |     | Beg. + NI - Draw    |     |   Ending Capital    |     | Operating/Invest/Fin|
+---------------------+     +---------------------+     +---------------------+     +---------------------+
```

1. **Income Statement**: Reports revenues and expenses and the resulting Net Income or Net Loss for a specific period of time.
2. **Owner's Equity Statement**: Summarizes changes in owner's equity for a specific period of time.
3. **Balance Sheet**: Reports assets, liabilities, and owner's equity at a specific point in time (snapshot date).
4. **Statement of Cash Flows**: Summarizes cash inflows (receipts) and cash outflows (payments) for a specific period of time across Operating, Investing, and Financing activities.

---

### 3.2 Articulation & Financial Statement Interrelationships
Financial statements are intrinsically linked through mathematical articulation:
* **Net Income** from the Income Statement flows directly into the Owner's Equity Statement as an addition to equity.
* **Ending Owner's Capital** calculated on the Owner's Equity Statement flows directly into the Owner's Equity section of the Balance Sheet.
* **Ending Cash** reported on the Balance Sheet MUST match the final ending cash balance on the Statement of Cash Flows.

---

### 3.3 Dr. Nadia's Master Rules for Income Statement Preparation

#### Rule 1: The 3-Line Heading (Mandatory Format)
* **Line 1 (WHO)**: Exact legal title of business or proprietor (e.g., *Nancy Grimwood, Attorney at Law*).
* **Line 2 (WHAT)**: *Income Statement*.
* **Line 3 (WHEN)**: Time period covered. Must state **`For the Month Ended [Date, Year]`** (or *For the Year Ended*).
  * ❌ **EXAM TRAP**: Never write a single date like "August 31, 2017" on an Income Statement!

#### Rule 2: Revenues First, Expenses Second
* **Revenues**: Listed first. Single revenue source goes straight to the total column; multiple revenues are detailed in the left subtotal column and summed in the right total column.
* **Expenses**: Listed second under the sub-heading **`Expenses:`** or **`Less Expenses:`**.
* **Magnitude Rule**: Individual expenses must be presented in **order of magnitude** (from largest dollar amount to smallest dollar amount).

#### Rule 3: Columnar Structure & Underlining Rules
* **Left Column**: Individual expense details.
* **Right Column**: Total Revenues, Total Expenses, and final Net Income / Net Loss.
* **Dollar Signs (\$)**: Placed on the first figure of each column and on the final Net Income figure.
* **Underlines**: Single underline above subtotal/total calculations; **double underline** beneath final Net Income / Net Loss.

#### Rule 4: Absolute Exclusion Rules
* ❌ **NO Balance Sheet Accounts**: Cash, Accounts Receivable, Supplies, Equipment, Accounts Payable, Notes Payable.
* ❌ **NO Equity Transactions**: Owner Investments, Owner Capital, Owner Drawings.

### 3.4 Dr. Nadia's Master Rules for Owner's Equity Statement Preparation

#### Rule 1: Heading Rules
* **Line 1**: Legal Company Name
* **Line 2**: *Owner's Equity Statement*
* **Line 3**: **`For the Month Ended [Date, Year]`** (Duration format)

4. **Subtractions (`Less:`)**: Owner's Drawings (and Net Loss if applicable).
5. **Ending Capital**: Labeled **`Owner's Capital, [Ending Date]`** with a double underline.

### 3.5 Dr. Nadia's Master Rules for Balance Sheet Preparation

#### Rule 1: The Single Snapshot Date Heading Rule
* **Line 1**: Legal Company Name
* **Line 2**: *Balance Sheet*
* **Line 3**: **`[Month Date, Year]`** (e.g., *August 31, 2017*).
  * ❌ **CRITICAL EXAM TRAP**: NEVER write "For the Month Ended..." on a Balance Sheet!

#### Rule 2: Asset Liquidity Ordering Rule
Assets must be presented in strict order of liquidity (ease of conversion to cash):
1. **Cash** (Always #1)
2. **Accounts Receivable**
3. **Supplies**
4. **Prepaid Insurance / Rent**
5. **Equipment / Land / Buildings** (Long-term assets last)

* **Liabilities**: Notes Payable (short-term formal debt) followed by Accounts Payable.
* **Owner's Equity**: **`[Owner Name], Capital`** MUST equal the **Ending Capital** figure from the Owner's Equity Statement.

#### Rule 4: Total Balancing Proof
Total Assets MUST equal Total Liabilities and Owner's Equity ($\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$). Both final totals receive a dollar sign (\$) and a **double underline**.

### 3.6 Statement of Cash Flows Framework
The Statement of Cash Flows classifies cash receipts and cash payments into three functional activity categories:

1. **Cash Flows from Operating Activities**: Cash effects of transactions that enter into the determination of Net Income.
   * *Inflows*: Cash collections from customers for services/goods.
   * *Outflows*: Cash payments to suppliers, employees, landlords, and utility companies.
2. **Cash Flows from Investing Activities**: Cash effects of purchasing and disposing of long-term operational assets.
   * *Inflows*: Cash received from sale of equipment/land.
   * *Outflows*: Cash paid to purchase equipment, machinery, supplies, or land.
3. **Cash Flows from Financing Activities**: Cash effects of owner capital contributions, borrowings, withdrawals, and debt repayments.
   * *Inflows*: Cash investments by owner, cash received from bank loan/Note Payable.
   * *Outflows*: Cash withdrawals by owner (Drawings), cash paid to settle loan principal.

## Section 5: The Double-Entry System, Debit/Credit Rules & Recording Process

### 5.1 The Account and T-Account Mechanics
An **account** is an individual accounting record of increases and decreases in a specific asset, liability, or owner's equity item. In its simplest basic form, an account is structured as a **T-account**:

```text
                           Account Title
                -----------------------------------
                     Debit (Dr.)  |  Credit (Cr.)
                      Left Side   |   Right Side
                -----------------------------------
```

* **Debit (Dr.)**: Entering an amount on the **left side** of an account.
* **Credit (Cr.)**: Entering an amount on the **right side** of an account.
* **Net Balance**: Determined by netting total debits against total credits.
  * If $\sum \text{Debits} > \sum \text{Credits} \rightarrow$ **Debit Balance**.
  * If $\sum \text{Credits} > \sum \text{Debits} \rightarrow$ **Credit Balance**.

---

### 5.2 Double-Entry Accounting System Rules
1. Every business transaction must affect **two or more accounts** to maintain accounting equation balance.
2. Every transaction requires at least one **Debit** entry and at least one **Credit** entry.
3. For every transaction, **Total Debits MUST Equal Total Credits** ($\sum \text{Debits} = \sum \text{Credits}$).

---

### 5.3 Golden Debit/Credit Rules & Normal Balances

The normal balance of any account is always on the side where increases are recorded.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        SUMMARY OF DEBIT / CREDIT RULES                            |
+----------------------+-------------------+-------------------+--------------------+
| Account Type         | Increase Side     | Decrease Side     | Normal Balance     |
+----------------------+-------------------+-------------------+--------------------+
| ASSETS               | DEBIT  (Dr.)  (+) | CREDIT (Cr.)  (-) | DEBIT  (Dr.)       |
| LIABILITIES          | CREDIT (Cr.)  (+) | DEBIT  (Dr.)  (-) | CREDIT (Cr.)       |
| OWNER'S CAPITAL      | CREDIT (Cr.)  (+) | DEBIT  (Dr.)  (-) | CREDIT (Cr.)       |
| OWNER'S DRAWINGS     | DEBIT  (Dr.)  (+) | CREDIT (Cr.)  (-) | DEBIT  (Dr.)       |
| REVENUES             | CREDIT (Cr.)  (+) | DEBIT  (Dr.)  (-) | CREDIT (Cr.)       |
| EXPENSES             | DEBIT  (Dr.)  (+) | CREDIT (Cr.)  (-) | DEBIT  (Dr.)       |
+----------------------+-------------------+-------------------+--------------------+
```

#### Memory Anchors
* **ADE**: **A**ssets, **D**rawings, **E**xpenses increase with a **DEBIT** (Normal Balance = Debit).
* **LCR**: **L**iabilities, **C**apital, **R**evenues increase with a **CREDIT** (Normal Balance = Credit).

---

### 5.4 The Four-Step Accounting Recording Process

```
+------------------+      +------------------+      +------------------+      +------------------+
| 1. TRANSACTION   | ---> |  2. JOURNALIZE   | ---> |     3. POST      | ---> | 4. TRIAL BALANCE |
|     ANALYSIS     |      |  General Journal |      | General Ledger   |      | Prove Dr. = Cr.  |
| Source Documents |      |  Chronological   |      |   (T-Accounts)   |      | Equality         |
+------------------+      +------------------+      +------------------+      +------------------+
```

#### Step 1: Transaction Analysis
Examine business source documents (invoices, sales slips, checks, cash receipts) to determine which accounts are affected and whether to debit or credit them.

#### Step 2: Journalizing in the General Journal
Transactions are entered chronologically in the **General Journal** before being posted to ledger accounts.
* **Journalizing Rules**:
  1. Date entered in Date column.
  2. **Debit Account** title entered flush against left margin. Debit amount entered in Debit column.
  3. **Credit Account** title indented 1 inch to the right on the line below. Credit amount entered in Credit column.
  4. Brief explanation written in parentheses on the line immediately below.

##### Sample General Journal Entry
| Date | Account Titles and Explanation | Ref. | Debit (\$) | Credit (\$) |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **2017 Oct. 1** | Cash | 101 | 10,000 | |
| | \quad Owner's Capital | 301 | | 10,000 |
| | *(Owner invested cash in business)* | | | |

#### Step 3: Posting to the General Ledger (T-Accounts)
Posting is the procedure of transferring journal entry amounts from the General Journal to individual accounts in the **General Ledger**.
* Ledger accounts maintain historical transaction details and calculate current running balances for each account.

#### Step 4: Preparing the Trial Balance
A **Trial Balance** is a formatted list of all ledger accounts and their balances at a given snapshot date, presented in ledger order (Assets, Liabilities, Owner's Capital, Drawings, Revenues, Expenses).

##### Primary Purposes
1. Proves the mathematical equality of total debits and total credits after posting ($\sum \text{Dr.} = \sum \text{Cr.}$).
2. Serves as the primary working foundation for preparing formal financial statements.

##### Sample Trial Balance Format
```text
                          PIONEER ADVERTISING
                             Trial Balance
                           October 31, 2017

                                                    Debit          Credit
   Cash .......................................   $15,200
   Supplies ...................................     2,500
   Prepaid Insurance ..........................       600
   Equipment ..................................     5,000
   Notes Payable ..............................                   $ 5,000
   Accounts Payable ...........................                     2,500
   Unearned Service Revenue ...................                     1,200
   Owner's Capital ............................                    10,000
   Owner's Drawings ...........................       500
   Service Revenue ............................                    10,000
   Salaries and Wages Expense .................     4,000
   Rent Expense ...............................       900
                                                  -------         -------
      Totals ..................................   $28,700         $28,700
                                                  =======         =======
```

##### Errors Not Uncovered by the Trial Balance
A Trial Balance may balance ($\sum \text{Dr.} = \sum \text{Cr.}$) even when errors exist! The Trial Balance fails to detect:
1. A transaction that was completely omitted from journalizing.
2. A correct journal entry that was posted twice.
3. A journal entry posted to the wrong account (e.g., debiting Equipment instead of Supplies).
4. Erroneous dollar amounts entered for both debit and credit equally.
5. Offsetting errors made in recording transaction amounts.

## Section 6: Master Formula Summary, Glossary & Midterm Exam Diagnostic Checklist

### 6.1 Master Mathematical Formula Matrix

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Basic Accounting Equation:} \quad & \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Equity} \\[6pt]
\text{Expanded Accounting Equation:} \quad & \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Capital} - \text{Owner's Drawings} + \text{Revenues} - \text{Expenses} \\[6pt]
\text{Net Income / (Net Loss):} \quad & \text{Net Income} = \text{Total Revenues} - \text{Total Expenses} \\[6pt]
\text{Ending Owner's Capital:} \quad & \text{Ending Capital} = \text{Beginning Capital} + \text{Owner Investments} + \text{Net Income} - \text{Owner Drawings} \\[6pt]
\text{Working Capital:} \quad & \text{Working Capital} = \text{Current Assets} - \text{Current Liabilities}
\end{aligned}$$

---

### 6.2 Key Accounting Terminology Glossary

1. **Account**: An individual accounting record of increases and decreases in a specific asset, liability, or owner's equity item.
2. **Accounts Payable**: Liabilities created when a business buys goods or services on credit.
3. **Accounts Receivable**: Assets representing claims against customers for services rendered or goods sold on credit.
4. **Assets**: Resources owned by a business that provide future economic benefits.
5. **Balance Sheet**: Financial statement reporting assets, liabilities, and owner's equity at a specific date.
6. **Chart of Accounts**: A list of all account titles and their assigned account numbers in the general ledger.
7. **Double-Entry System**: Accounting system where each transaction affects two or more accounts and total debits equal total credits.
8. **Economic Entity Assumption**: Assumption requiring business activities to be kept distinct from personal activities of the owner.
9. **Expenses**: Cost of assets consumed or services used in the process of earning revenue.
10. **Fair Value Principle**: Accounting principle stating that assets and liabilities should be reported at fair value.
11. **FASB**: Financial Accounting Standards Board; primary U.S. private-sector standard-setting body establishing GAAP.
12. **Financial Accounting**: Branch of accounting focused on reporting financial information to external decision-makers.
13. **GAAP**: Generally Accepted Accounting Principles; rules and standards universally practiced in financial reporting.
14. **General Journal**: Chronological accounting record where transactions are initially recorded before posting.
15. **General Ledger**: Record containing all asset, liability, owner's equity, revenue, and expense accounts.
16. **Going Concern Assumption**: Assumption that a company will remain in operation for the foreseeable future.
17. **Historical Cost Principle**: Accounting principle dictating that assets be recorded and maintained at acquisition cost.
18. **IASB**: International Accounting Standards Board; independent body establishing IFRS worldwide.
19. **IFRS**: International Financial Reporting Standards; accounting standards used in over 100 countries.
20. **Income Statement**: Financial statement reporting revenues, expenses, and net income or loss for a period of time.
21. **Liabilities**: Debts and obligations owed to creditors.
22. **Monetary Unit Assumption**: Assumption requiring that only money-expressible transaction data be recorded.
23. **Normal Balance**: The side of an account (debit or credit) where increases are recorded.
24. **Owner's Capital**: Asset contributions made into the enterprise by the owner.
25. **Owner's Drawings**: Cash or asset withdrawals made by the owner for personal living use (disinvestment).
26. **Owner's Equity**: Residual ownership claim on total assets ($\text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}$).
27. **Posting**: Procedure of transferring journal entries to general ledger accounts.
28. **Revenues**: Gross increases in owner's equity resulting from business operations for earning income.
29. **T-Account**: Simplified graphic representation of an account with a title, debit (left) side, and credit (right) side.
30. **Trial Balance**: Formatted list of ledger accounts and their balances at a given time to prove debit/credit equality.

### 6.3 Midterm Exam Common Traps & Diagnostic Checklist

* [ ] **Heading Date Trap**: Verify that Income Statement, Owner's Equity Statement, and Cash Flow Statement headings state **`For the Month Ended [Date]`**, while the Balance Sheet heading states **only the single snapshot date** (`August 31, 2017`).
* [ ] **Unearned Revenue Classification Trap**: Remember that **Unearned Service Revenue is a Liability**, NOT a revenue account! (Normal Balance = Credit).
* [ ] **Prepaid Insurance / Rent Classification Trap**: Remember that **Prepaid Insurance / Rent is an Asset**, NOT an expense account! (Normal Balance = Debit).
* [ ] **Owner Drawings Placement Trap**: Never include Owner Drawings on the Income Statement! Drawings are a direct equity subtraction on the Owner's Equity Statement.
* [ ] **Expense Magnitude Rule**: Ensure expenses on the Income Statement are listed in order of dollar magnitude (largest to smallest) with Total Expenses in the right-hand column.
* [ ] **Journal Entry Indentation**: Ensure credited account titles are indented 1 inch to the right in the General Journal.
* [ ] **Balance Sheet Capital Source**: Ensure the Owner's Capital line on the Balance Sheet uses the **Ending Capital** calculated from the Owner's Equity Statement, NOT the beginning capital or initial investment figure.
* [ ] **Trial Balance Limitations**: Remember that a balancing Trial Balance ($\text{Total Debits} = \text{Total Credits}$) does NOT guarantee zero errors (omitted transactions, wrong account debits/credits, or double postings will not throw the Trial Balance off balance).
